Every day was the same.
I just hadn't noticed.

StateShift didn't begin with coffee. It began with paternity leave, an infant daughter, and the slow realisation that I'd been sleeping through my own life.

The month I stopped noticing

Somewhere in there I lost the ability to tell one week from the next. Not because anything was wrong. Because nothing was.

Groundhog Day, except Bill Murray at least knew he was stuck.

Then my daughter was born and I stepped away from work. Suddenly the day had seams in it.

One hour I was a father, holding an infant who has no opinion about my inbox. The next I was at a laptop, trying to think in straight lines. Then a son. Then a husband. Then, briefly, just a person who wanted twenty minutes that belonged to nobody.

We all wear these hats. We all want to be good at every one of them. But nobody gives you a moment between them. You are expected to simply switch, so you don't. You carry the last hour into the next one and end up half-present in both.

What I needed wasn't more energy. It was a door between the rooms.

It started with rain

That April it rained properly for the first time in months. I stepped outside and the smell of wet earth put me, instantly and without asking, in a courtyard I had not stood in for twenty-five years.

That is the whole idea. Nothing moves you between states faster than smell. Not caffeine, not a to-do list. Aroma arrives before thought does.

So I stopped reading about coffee and started reading about perfume. A good perfume never smells the same from beginning to end. It unfolds. Top notes that catch you, heart notes that hold you, a base that stays long after you have left the room. I wanted to know whether a cup could be composed the same way. A beginning, a middle, and an after.

But an aroma you can't drink is just a candle. The coffee had to be good, and good coffee in India usually means a café, a queue and four hundred rupees, or a shelf of equipment you will use twice and resent forever. Neither of those is a transition. Both are errands.

So the format became part of the design. Two sachets, hot water, three minutes. One holds the coffee. One holds everything the aroma needs. No machine, no skill, no leaving the house.

A kitchen counter, a scale, and a great deal of bad coffee

All of it happens at a kitchen counter in North India: a hand grinder, a 0.1-gram scale, and a notebook that no longer closes properly.

Mostly it has been failure. Cardamom that disappeared into milk. Cardamom that turned the cup into chai. Spice that tasted bright on Monday and bitter by Friday, because volatile oils do not care about your schedule. Sachets that leaked. Cups my wife described, with devastating economy, as plain. Once, memorably, as half-cooked.

The rules came out of the failures, not the plan. Grind fresh, every time. Nothing sits. Nothing gets rescued with sugar. If an ingredient can't earn its place at a tenth of a gram, it doesn't get in at all. More have been retired than kept.

The notebook · May 25 "aroma survived the milk. she found the cardamom without being asked. breakthrough."

Then one May morning she named the cardamom from across the kitchen. Unprompted. That was the first cup that worked, and everything since, failures especially, is in the journal.

What we believe

Four rules we don't break

Coffee is the medium, not the product

We are not trying to sell you a better bean. We're trying to give you back three minutes you already own.

Slow first, then fast

A cup should slow you down before it speeds you up. The pause before the lift isn't a delay. It's the entire point.

A cup that fits everywhere fits nowhere

Each blend is made for one part of the day, and honest about which. If it works equally well at 7am and 10pm, it wasn't designed. It was averaged.

The pause is the recipe

Ingredients can be copied. Three unhurried minutes, taken deliberately, cannot. We design for the wait as carefully as the taste.

I would rather make something a few hundred people love
than something ten thousand people quite like.
Mandhir
